The bread dough on my grandmother's counter had stopped rising, and she was blaming the weather. I was eleven and I did not believe her. I opened her notebook, the one with flour thumbprints on every page, and started reading her entries backwards. Three weeks earlier she had written "new yeast, from the corner store." Every failed loaf came after that line.

I told her. She laughed, then looked at the page for a long time. She had been baking for sixty years and had never once compared one week with another.

That afternoon I made my first chart: dates down the side, flour brand, yeast brand, room temperature, result. We baked eleven loaves in two weeks. I labelled the bags with a marker and kept a thermometer on the windowsill. The old yeast won every time, and my grandmother ordered a case of it by mail.

I did not decide that day to study statistics. What I decided was smaller. I started writing down what happened before I explained it. In chemistry I now keep a log of every titration, including the ones I ruined by reading the burette wrong. When our robotics team lost a match, I was the one asking for the match video and a pencil.

My grandmother still bakes on Sundays. Last month she handed me the notebook and said the next page was mine.
